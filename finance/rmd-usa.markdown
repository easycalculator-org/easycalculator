<section class="rmd-panel"><div class="rmd-step">Understand your result</div><h2>How is an RMD calculated?</h2><p class="rmd-muted">An RMD is the minimum you generally must withdraw from an eligible retirement account each year after reaching your starting age.</p><div class="rmd-formula">Prior December 31 balance ÷ IRS factor</div><p class="rmd-muted">Example: at age 73, the Uniform Lifetime factor is 26.5. A $500,000 balance gives an annual RMD of <strong>$18,867.93</strong> when rounded up to the next cent.</p><h3>When do withdrawals begin?</h3><table><caption class="rmd-help">Age-based starting rules for original owners</caption><thead><tr><th scope="col">Date of birth</th><th scope="col">Starting age</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Before July 1, 1949</td><td>70½</td></tr><tr><td>July 1, 1949–December 31, 1950</td><td>72</td></tr><tr><td>1951–1958</td><td>73</td></tr><tr><td>1959</td><td>73*</td></tr><tr><td>1960 or later</td><td>75</td></tr></tbody></table><p class="rmd-help">*The 1959 birth-year treatment uses the IRS proposed clarification (age 73); confirm final guidance before the first distribution year. Employer-plan retirement exceptions can change the starting date.</p><p class="rmd-note">For the first age-based RMD year, you can generally wait until April 1 of the next year. That next year’s own RMD is still due by December 31, so delaying can mean two RMDs in one calendar year.</p></section>
<section class="rmd-panel"><div class="rmd-step">IRS reference</div><h2>Uniform Lifetime Table</h2><p class="rmd-muted">Your age selects the factor. A smaller factor means a larger percentage to withdraw.</p><div class="rmd-table-wrap" tabindex="0" role="region" aria-label="Scrollable IRS factor table"><table><thead><tr><th scope="col">Age</th><th scope="col">Factor</th><th scope="col">Rate</th></tr></thead><tbody id="rmd-table"></tbody></table></div><p class="rmd-help">Table III, IRS Publication 590-B. Ages 120 and over use 2.0. The table applies only when the younger-spouse exception does not apply.</p></section>

<section class="rmd-panel" id="rmd-faq" style="margin-top:22px"><h2>Common questions</h2><details><summary>Can I combine several retirement accounts?</summary><p>Calculate each account’s RMD separately. Generally, traditional IRA RMDs can be totaled and taken from one or more of your IRAs. A similar rule applies within 403(b) accounts. Other employer plans, such as 401(k)s, generally require separate withdrawals. Do not combine unlike account types in this calculator.</p></details><details><summary>Do Roth accounts have RMDs?</summary><p>Original owners do not have lifetime RMDs for Roth IRAs or designated Roth employer-plan accounts under current rules. Inherited Roth accounts have separate beneficiary rules.</p></details><details><summary>What if I have already withdrawn more than the RMD?</summary><p>Your remaining amount is zero. An excess withdrawal does not count toward next year’s RMD. Only enter withdrawals that qualify toward the selected year’s requirement.</p></details><details><summary>Is the result my tax bill?</summary><p>No. It is an estimated gross withdrawal requirement. Taxable amounts depend on basis, qualified charitable distributions and other circumstances. This calculator does not estimate income tax or penalties.</p></details><details><summary>Which situations need a different calculation?</summary><p>Inherited accounts, a sole-beneficiary spouse more than 10 years younger, retirement-delayed employer plans, annuities and special balance adjustments can require different treatment. Confirm those calculations with your custodian or tax professional.</p></details></section>
